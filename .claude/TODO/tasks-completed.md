### Club governance

- [x] **A club's official email is seeded at creation** — **done 2026-09-10.** Arpan asked for it immediately after the contact links shipped, and for club owners and admins to be able to edit it. Put to him with the consequence — editing it is what makes it a trust anchor, and [`club_admin_governance.md`](../docs/architecture/club_admin_governance.md) §6 holds the address outside the control of whoever currently controls the club precisely so a takeover cannot repoint recovery at itself — he chose **seed it, keep editing platform-admin-only**. So no owner/club-admin edit UI was built, and none should be.

  Until this, `official_email` could not be set until the club already existed: `ClubCreateRequest` carried no field for it, deliberately, and `PATCH /clubs/{clubId}/official-email` was the only writer. Every club therefore started with no recovery address at all. Both paths now seed it from the contact email the form already collects — the admin path in `ClubController.create`, the proposal path in `ClubCreationRequestService.approve`, which reads it out of the proposal's stored `social_links` rather than keeping a second copy that could disagree.

  **Seeded, not merged** — Arpan, 2026-09-10. `social_links.email` and `official_email` are separate columns with separate rules from the moment the club exists, so editing the public contact address never moves the recovery one. [ADR-006](../docs/decisions/ADR-006-official-email-verified-only-by-round-trip.md) is untouched: a seeded address is unverified like any other, and at creation there is no stamp to inherit or clear. The `ClubCreateRequest` note that said there is deliberately no such field is amended rather than deleted — its reasoning is about a club that already has a stamp, and never reached creation.

  **No migration and no new dashboard.** The column has existed since V13, and `/manage/[clubId]` already showed the address and its verified state to the whole management team while drawing the edit control for a platform admin alone. The `CLUB_CREATED` audit entry now carries the seeded address, so the log answers who pointed a club's recovery channel where it points. Four ITs and six frontend tests, including `clubService.test.ts` — new, because the form tests stop at `createClubWithMedia` and everything below it could drop a field with the suite green.

- [x] **P1** *(items 5 and 7)* **Invite and remove club admins** — **done 2026-08-18.** The owner-facing half of the Administrators tab, which until now listed people and offered no actions. `V15__invite_club_admins_by_email.sql` drops `NOT NULL` from `club_admin_assignments.user_id` and adds `invited_email`, so an invitation can name an address with no CampusVibe account yet — the incoming treasurer is routinely not signed up in August, and a user picker could only have offered accounts that already exist. Two CHECK constraints keep that from weakening anything: a row must name somebody, and an ACTIVE row must name an *account* rather than a mailbox, because `ClubPermissionService` looks up `(club_id, user_id)` and could not evaluate a permission granted to a string of text. A partial unique index on `(club_id, lower(invited_email))` is the email-side twin of `one_live_assignment_per_club_user`, which cannot cover invitations to strangers — its key is `user_id`, and NULLs are distinct in a PostgreSQL unique index, so a hundred invitations to one unknown address would sit there without colliding.

  **`AuthTokenService` was deliberately *not* extended, which is what the queued task proposed.** Acceptance is an authenticated `POST` as the invitee instead: the session proves identity, where a mailed token proves only that whoever holds it read the message. That is strictly stronger — nothing forwardable, nothing resident in an inbox — and it avoids needing a context column on `auth_tokens`, which `issue()` would otherwise require to stop one club's invitation deleting another club's invitation to the same person.

  **The security boundary is `ClubAdminService.claimableBy`,** which refuses an account whose address is unconfirmed. Sign-up does not require confirming one (`campusvibe.auth.require-verified-email` is off by default), so without it the attack is: read a club's published exec list, register the incoming treasurer's address before they do, and wait for the owner to send the invitation to you. Declining is held to the same rule — otherwise an unconfirmed squatter could quietly keep a rival off a club's team.

  Removal and cancelling an invitation are one endpoint, because they are one act on one row; the status it was in only decides which email goes out. Owner rows are refused to everybody, platform admins included (§36), so the club is never left without an owner — that is what transfer is for. The row becomes REVOKED and stays, per §7. Addressed by assignment id rather than the `{userId}` §33 sketches, since an invitation to a stranger has no user id and still has to be cancellable; the club in the path is checked against the row so an owner of one club cannot revoke in another by sending its id.

  Frontend: the invite form and per-row remove control on `/manage/[clubId]/admins`, a new `/invitations` page where the email lands, and a counted `Invitations` link in the navbar — someone invited before they manage anything has no other route in, since `/manage` is hidden from them. Invitations moved onto `ManagedClubsProvider`, fetched with `Promise.allSettled` alongside the managed clubs so an unreadable invitation list cannot make the app believe the user manages nothing.

- [x] **P2** *(item 8)* **Ownership transfer** — **done 2026-08-18.** An owner could not leave a club, hand it on, or do anything about graduating; the Administrators tab promised this in two places. `V16__create_club_ownership_transfers.sql` holds a handover while it waits to be answered. It could not be a PENDING `CLUB_OWNER` row in `club_admin_assignments`: the successor must already be an ACTIVE `CLUB_ADMIN`, and `one_live_assignment_per_club_user` is UNIQUE `(club_id, user_id)` over live rows, so a second live row for the same person in the same club is exactly what that index forbids. Relaxing it to include `role` was the alternative and was rejected — "one live relationship per person per club" is the property the invitation logic leans on.

  **Eligibility is deliberately narrow: an active admin of that club, chosen from a dropdown.** They have already accepted an invitation, which means they proved their address and agreed to be involved. Offering the club to an arbitrary address would collapse "should this person help run the club" and "should they own it" into a single click.

  **The outgoing owner chooses whether they stay**, per §8, defaulting to staying an admin — the common case is a handover inside a continuing exec team. Stored on the transfer row rather than asked at acceptance, because it is the outgoing owner's decision about their own membership and the incoming owner should not be making it.

  **`accept` is the only call in the application that moves authority between two people,** so it is one transaction (§26) and everything in it is re-read rather than trusted from when the offer was made: an offer can sit for days, and the successor may have been removed or the owner may have changed since. Inside it, the demotion is flushed before the promotion, because `one_active_owner_per_club` is a partial unique index and PostgreSQL checks those per statement — a partial index cannot be DEFERRABLE, so there is no instant in which two rows may both be owners. **That ordering was measured, not assumed:** removing both flushes still passes the whole suite today, because Hibernate happens to emit the updates in the order they were dirtied. It is not a documented guarantee, and the failure mode would be a constraint violation on an index nobody would suspect, so the flushes stay and the comment says exactly this.

  Removing an admin now also cancels a handover offered to them — otherwise it sits PENDING for someone who cannot accept, holding the club's single transfer slot. The three club-scoped endpoints take no transfer id, since `one_pending_transfer_per_club` guarantees there is at most one to address.

  Frontend: a `TransferOwnershipPanel` at the foot of the Administrators tab, behind a disclosure and styled as a warning because it is the one action there the club cannot undo by itself; and an `OwnershipCard` on `/invitations`, rendered above admin invitations and visibly heavier, saying what happens to the outgoing owner too.

  23 tests in `ClubOwnershipTransferIT` (suite 153 → 176, still the one pre-existing `SearchIT` failure). Verified live in both dispositions: stay-as-admin and leave-entirely, each ending with exactly one active owner and with pre-existing bearer tokens gaining and losing the right powers immediately.

  **`club_audit_logs` (V17) is append-only, enforced by a `BEFORE UPDATE OR DELETE` trigger** that raises an exception. §22 asks that a club administrator cannot delete the entry recording what they did, and repository discipline was the alternative — true until someone adds a delete, and the person likeliest to add one is the person the rule constrains. `TRUNCATE` still works, because it fires statement-level triggers only; that is the deliberate escape hatch for resetting a test database, and nothing in the application issues it.

A key would make the history only as durable as the thing it describes, and a club being deleted is the moment its history is most worth having. For the same reason the actor's name is *snapshotted* rather than joined: the line has to still read correctly after the account is renamed or deleted, and "Sarah removed Michael" should say what it said that day. Covered by `theActorsNameIsFrozenAtTheMoment` and `theLogSurvivesTheClubItDescribes`.

  Eight actions across the administration and ownership lifecycles, written through one `ClubAuditService` per §35 — scattered inserts drift in what they record and cannot be changed consistently later. **Writes join the caller's transaction** rather than opening their own: an action that happened without being logged is a hole in the record, one logged without happening is a lie, and §26 already puts the audit write inside the transfer transaction. The cost is that a bug here can fail a user action, which is the right way round for a governance log.

  The Activity entry is now in `ManageSidebar`, last, because it records what already happened — where you go after noticing something, not where you start. Paged with a keyset cursor on `id`, not an offset and not `created_at`: two actions inside one transaction share a timestamp to the microsecond, and an offset repeats a row whenever an entry arrives between two page requests. The frontend words each entry, so fixing a sentence is not an API redeploy, and it has a fallback branch for an action the build does not recognise.

### Backend / Features

- [x] **P1** **User profiles have a backend** — **done 2026-08-20.** `/profile/edit` had shipped the day before as a complete UI over memory: `saveProfile` and `saveNotificationPreferences` resolved without calling anything, so every section saved, confirmed *Changes saved*, and was gone on reload. Roughly 1,900 lines of frontend rested on a persistence layer that did not exist. Four migrations (V18–V21), three tables plus a reference table, six endpoints, and the frontend seams the stub was built to leave. Written up in [`user-profiles.md`](../docs/architecture/user-profiles.md).

  **The decision everything else followed from: the write is a full-replace `PUT`, not a `PATCH`.** Three editor screens each own a slice of one profile — bio and links, degree and subjects, interests — and each submits a *whole* `UserProfile`. So what does *Program info* sending `bio: null` mean? A PATCH would have to tell an absent key from an explicit null, which a Java record cannot do without wrapping every component in an `Optional`; and a PATCH where null means *untouched* would make clearing a bio impossible, which people legitimately want. PUT makes null mean *clear this* and nothing else. The cost is that the client must hold the whole profile before it may save.

  **Which is why `ProfileProvider` exists, and it is a correctness fix rather than a performance one.** It loads the profile once at `(protected)/profile/layout.tsx`, above both the profile page and all five settings sections. Without it, every section seeds its draft from `emptyProfile()` — harmless while nothing persisted, and the moment it does, saving on the program page writes `bio: null` over the bio typed on the page before. `savingReplacesEverythingRatherThanMerging` in `UserProfileIT` pins the server half; the invariant on the frontend can only be documented, so it is stated on `UserProfileUpdateRequest`, on `saveProfile`, and in the provider's own header.

  **The profile is its own table, keyed on `user_id` as its primary key.** Not columns on `users`: that row *is* the Spring Security principal and `JWTAuthenticationFilter` re-loads it on every authenticated request, so a bio would make every request in the system pay for a column two screens read. A shared primary key says *exactly one, no identity of its own* in the schema rather than in a comment plus a unique index. Rows are created on first write, never at sign-up — every existing account predates the table, so absence has to be a supported state regardless, and creating it early would give a profile two ways to come into being.

  **Interests get a foreign key; subjects, in the same form, are free text.** An interest exists so that two people who both picked it are discoverable as the same thing, so `Board games`, `board games` and `Boardgames` cannot be three groups of one — the value has to come from a shared list. A subject is never matched against anything, and a closed list missing your programme tells you your programme does not exist. The catalogue (76 entries, V20) is **keyed on a slug, not the label**: the labels have had no product review and renames are likely, and with a label key renaming `LGBTQ+` would have to rewrite every referencing row inside the same migration or orphan them. Its FK is `ON DELETE RESTRICT` where the profile's is `CASCADE` — deleting a profile should take its choices with it, retiring an interest should *not* silently delete it from everyone who picked it.

  **`INTEREST_CATEGORIES` was deleted from the frontend** and the picker now reads `GET /api/v1/interests`. Keeping a copy would have been two vocabularies that must agree with nothing checking that they do — the exact failure `contracts/api-dto-fields.json` exists to prevent, and not worth reintroducing one directory over. `DEGREES` and `MCGILL_FACULTIES` stayed: nothing stores a foreign key to a degree, and faculties get renamed, which a `CHECK` constraint would turn into a migration that has to ship before the frontend can.

  **Email preferences are a third table with their own endpoint.** They are not profile content: a profile is what other students see, preferences are what the mail path may send, and the mail path has no business loading a bio to read five booleans. A read deliberately does *not* create a row — persisting on read would freeze today's defaults into rows for people who never touched a switch, so changing a default later would only affect accounts that had never looked. That makes the entity's field initialisers the *meaning* of an absent row, and V21's column defaults and the editor's initial state have to agree with them; all three say everything on except `productNews`.

  **A social link is checked in two places, and the ordering inside each is the security property.** `normaliseProfileLink` runs in the browser and is therefore not a control — anything holding a token can PUT past it; `ProfileLinks.normalise` is. Both refuse any scheme but http/https, and both check the scheme *before* assuming https for a bare `instagram.com/someone`. Reversed, `javascript:alert(1)` becomes `https://javascript:alert(1)` — an https URL that passes a scheme check and reaches an `href`. Both sides carry a test named after that ordering. The browser copy stays because it guards the *render*, and a row written before the server rule existed would still render.

  **Four DTOs joined the contract, and `ProfileSocialLinksDTO` is not redundant.** The contract records a nested object as a *single field name* — `UserProfileDTO` contributes `socialLinks` and says nothing about what is inside it, exactly as `MyEventDTO` contributes `event` — so without an entry of its own, renaming `instagram` on one side would break the app with both suites green. Pinning it meant extracting the anonymous inline type in `types/index.ts` into a named `ProfileSocialLinks`, because `Record<keyof T, true>` needs a name to key on. `UserDTO` gained nothing, as the frontend type's own comment insists.

  **Three seams had to be widened.** `useEditableForm` gained `reinitialise(next)` — `initial` is only read on the first render, so a form that mounts before its data arrives had no way to pick it up; it is distinct from `commit`, which promotes what the user typed. `AuthContext` gained `refreshUser()` and `applyUser(user)`, because `user` is fetched once on mount and renaming yourself would otherwise leave the navbar showing the old name until a reload. And `SaveChangesBar` now shows what the server said instead of a fixed sentence: the refusals this screen provokes are specific and actionable — a link that is not http, an interest outside the catalogue, too many subjects — and *try again in a moment* is wrong for all of them, because trying again unchanged fails again.

  **The name is the one exception to the PUT.** It lives on `users`, so it is written by `PATCH /api/v1/users/me` — genuinely a patch, one named field, the rest of the account untouched. The Edit profile screen makes two calls and only makes the first when the name actually changed. This also retired `UserUpdateRequest`, which had been dead scaffolding since it was written: two private fields, no accessors, no references.

Three controls, because a rate limit alone still lets a slow steady caller spend indefinitely: a per-IP budget on `/events/search` and `/clubs/search` (30/min, its own budget separate from auth — different reason, different numbers), a 200-character query cap rejected with 400 rather than truncated (truncating still pays for the work and answers a different question), and `QueryEmbeddingCache` in front of the provider call so identical repeat queries cost one embedding instead of N. Off under the `test` profile so the suite does not lock itself out; `AuthRateLimitIT` turns it back on with its own limits. 9 tests, plus a live probe. **Two accepted limits, one trigger:** counters are per instance and reset on restart — move to Redis when a second instance exists. **Production behind an ALB must set `AUTH_RATE_LIMIT_TRUST_XFF=true`**, or every caller shares one bucket.
- [x] **P1** ~~**Password reset, end to end**~~ — **done 2026-08-15.** `auth_tokens` (V11), `POST /forgot-password` + `POST /reset-password`, and a real `ResetPasswordView` behind `/?auth=reset-password&token=…`. Always 204 from `forgot-password` — unknown address, Google account and mail outage are indistinguishable — and the frontend swallows errors to keep it that way. A Google account is sent nothing: it has no password, and mailing a link would turn a mailbox compromise into a way to bolt one on. Redeeming also verifies the address and clears the lockout counter. 12 tests.
- [x] **P2** ~~**Email verification on signup**~~ — **done 2026-08-15.** Sent on register, redeemed at `/?auth=verify-email&token=…`, resendable by a signed-in user. **The gate is OFF** (`AUTH_REQUIRE_VERIFIED_EMAIL=false`): the link works but nothing depends on it yet, because requiring it is a product call — it stops throwaway addresses at the cost of locking out anyone whose mail never arrives. When on, an unverified sign-in is 403 with its own message, not 401. 14 tests.

- [x] **P2** ~~**Add an `auth_provider` column and make `password` nullable**~~ — **done 2026-08-15**, `V10__auth_provider.sql`. Three check constraints: provider is LOCAL or GOOGLE, LOCAL has a password hash and GOOGLE has none, email is always lowercase. The fake-password hack is gone. Came with `GET /api/v1/auth/email-status` and the signup-time hint that tells a Google user to continue with Google rather than refusing them after they have filled in the form, plus case-insensitive email matching throughout. 18 new integration tests in `AuthProviderIT`. **Rows created before V10 stay `LOCAL`** — the old placeholder hashes are bcrypt output and cannot be told from real ones.

Login keeps its identical-401 behaviour, so the disclosure is confined to the one endpoint the product needs it on. **Unbounded until rate limiting lands** — that is the mitigation, and it is the next item.
